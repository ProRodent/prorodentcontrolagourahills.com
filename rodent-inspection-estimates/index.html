What happens in the first minutes.

The technician starts by asking you to retell the story: what you saw, when, and where. That account is more valuable than it feels, because rodents leave patterns, and a good description narrows the search before anyone opens a hatch.

Next comes a lap around the outside. Eaves, vents, utility entries, the garage door, the fence line and any plantings touching the walls are checked and photographed. Then the interior inspection follows the same logic, working from the freshest sign outward.

Reading the evidence.

  • Droppings: size, color and quantity indicate species and how recently the animals were active.
  • Rub marks: greasy smears show routes and the height of travel.
  • Gnawing: tooth width and the shape of a hole point to mouse or rat.
  • Nesting: shredded insulation, paper or fabric shows where animals rest.
  • Odors: ammonia suggests living animals, while a sweet rot suggests a carcass.

Photographs from the visit go with the estimate. If a trap is needed, the plan shows where it will go and why.

Attics, crawl spaces and the places nobody visits.

An inspection is only as good as the access it gets. Clear a path to the attic hatch and tell the technician about low clearance, loose boards or old insulation you already suspect. Where a space is unsafe to enter, the estimate says so, and the technician looks at it from the best safe position.

Some homes near hillsides and planted slopes have crawl spaces or sub-floor areas that stay damp or shaded. We have no survey of which ones do. If yours has one, say so on the call, because it is a place animals often favor.

Preparing the house.

Put pets in a closed room during the visit. Move parked cars so the technician can reach the garage walls. If you have already set traps or put out bait, tell us exactly where, so nobody reaches into the wrong spot.

A short list of questions to ask the technician helps too: how many entry points were found, what would you do first, and what happens if I skip a step? Honest answers to those three questions make the estimate easy to judge.

While the clock runs.

  • Snap the lids on the trash bins tonight and roll them away from the garage wall.
  • If you do clean, wet the spot with disinfectant first and use paper towels.
  • Raise pet dishes to a shelf for the night.
  • Look at the bottom edge of the garage door and describe any daylight you can see.

Handling droppings without raising dust.

Every item below matches the federal pages linked beneath this section, so you can check the method yourself.

  • Brooms, dusters and compressed air are out; a pump sprayer is in.
  • Make a draft with two openings, then busy yourself elsewhere until the time is up.
  • Choose gloves long enough to cover the wrist, where dust likes to land.
  • The disinfectant must sit before it works, so do not wipe at once.
  • Seal the bag before washing your hands.
  • Washing is the final step, whether or not you wore gloves.

Inspection visit FAQ.

How long does an inspection take?

Plan on the better part of an hour for a typical house, longer if the attic or crawl space is hard to reach. Large or complicated properties can take more.

Must someone be at home?

An adult should either be there for entry or leave clear access instructions. We like to have someone who can say when and where the signs showed up.

Will the estimate include everything?

It lists what the inspection found, each with its own price. Problems outside rodent work, such as a roof leak, are noted but not priced.

Can I get the inspection without the rest?

Yes. The written list is yours to keep, and no one is obliged to book the next step.

What if you find nothing?

Then the report says so, and it records where we looked. Sometimes the noise is a squirrel, a bird or pipes, and that is useful to know.
