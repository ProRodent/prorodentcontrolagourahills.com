Why this step comes last.

A cleanup done while animals are still coming and going is wasted effort. They simply foul the clean space again. For that reason sanitizing follows trapping and sealing, and it only begins when the technician has confirmed the structure is empty.

When the time comes, the work is methodical. Protective gear goes on, the space is ventilated and the contaminated material is lifted out wet and bagged. Only then can the underlying surfaces be treated.

What gets removed and what stays.

  • Bags receive pellets, urine-soaked insulation and nest mats.
  • Insulation that is stained or matted flat gets replaced.
  • Sound wood, ductwork and vapor barriers are cleaned where they sit.
  • Broken ducts are marked for repair and never covered over.

The estimate separates each of these so you can see what is removed and what is saved.

Homes with tile roofs, vaulted ceilings and tight attics.

Some houses in the area have attic spaces that are hard to enter, such as low-clearance crawlways above vaulted rooms. We have no survey of which ones do. When access is difficult, the estimate notes it, and the technician may need extra time and different equipment.

If you can reach the hatch, take photographs of what you see without entering. They help the technician plan before the visit.

What to do before the crew arrives.

  • Open a way to the hatch and have a sturdy ladder ready.
  • Hold children and pets back from the work zone.
  • Cover stored items you want to protect.
  • Note any insulation type you know of, such as batts, blown-in or foam.

When wiring has been gnawed, the repair page explains what happens next. The trapping page explains how animals are removed before cleanup begins.

Steps while waiting.

  • Snap the lids on the trash bins tonight and roll them away from the garage wall.
  • If you do clean, wet the spot with disinfectant first and use paper towels.
  • Raise pet dishes to a shelf for the night.
  • Look at the bottom edge of the garage door and describe any daylight you can see.

A wet-wipe routine for pellets and nests.

Many homeowners handle small cleanups themselves, even when a technician is on the way, so keep these precautions nearby.

  • A single dry pellet can crumble at a touch and release a cloud you cannot see.
  • Plan on half an hour of airing before any wiping begins.
  • Gloves belong on your hands before you touch the spray bottle.
  • Let the liquid sit for around five minutes; the wait does the disinfecting.
  • Seal the bag before washing your hands.
  • Scrub under the fingernails and up the wrists, then use a fresh towel.

Cleanup queries from callers.

Why not clean first and seal later?

Because animals that return soil the new insulation again. The order is remove, seal, then clean.

Can I do this myself?

Small areas, yes, following the CDC method on this page. Large attics need protective gear, a respirator and proper disposal, and we recommend a professional.

Is the dust dangerous?

Germs can ride on dry droppings and urine, and stirring them sends dust up. That is the reason for a wet, unhurried, protected method.

Will new insulation be needed?

Often, where animals nested or soiled it. The estimate shows each area that needs replacement.

How long does it take?

Small jobs finish in hours. Large attics can take a full day or more.

Preparing for a sanitizing visit.

Tell us how to get in, how tall the space is, and which patches of insulation look flat, dark or pushed into a corner. We clean nothing while animals still live there.
