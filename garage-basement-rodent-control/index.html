Why the garage draws them.

A garage is often the least-watched room in a house. The door opens and closes, the light stays off for long stretches, and the shelves collect cartons that no one touches for months. To a mouse or rat that is a quiet, sheltered pantry with an easy way in.

The bottom seal of the overhead door is the favorite gate. A rubber strip that has curled, split or flattened leaves a gap along the slab. Check it by looking for daylight while standing inside with the door closed.

A walk-through, wall by wall.

  • Start at the overhead door and look at both bottom corners, the rubber seal and the side tracks.
  • Check the walk-through door for a worn sweep or a gap at the threshold.
  • Follow the slab edge where the wall meets the floor for cracks or pull-away.
  • Look behind the water heater, the freezer and the laundry hookups.
  • Note any holes where pipes, conduit or cables pass through the wall.

The technician logs each point on a sketch so you can see which gaps are closest to the evidence.

A word on the shared wall.

When the garage is attached to the house, the wall between them often holds pipes and wiring that connect the two. A gap there becomes a short route from the garage into a stud bay and then the kitchen or laundry. If you hear noise in the garage and in the adjoining room at once, say so on the call.

A few tasks before the visit.

  • List every pet, including any cat that wanders outdoors.
  • Scrub up after handling anything near the mess, gloves or not.
  • Leave the foam can in the garage; stuffing a hole hides the gap we need to measure.
  • Peek under sinks and behind the water heater with a flashlight and report back.

Read this before you clean.

Hantavirus and other germs can ride on dust from dried waste, and that is why the method below avoids anything that stirs it up.

  • Health agencies tell people not to sweep or vacuum dry rodent waste, so we repeat the warning here.
  • Make a draft with two openings, then busy yourself elsewhere until the time is up.
  • Choose gloves long enough to cover the wrist, where dust likes to land.
  • The disinfectant must sit before it works, so do not wipe at once.
  • Two bags keep the waste contained until pickup.
  • Rinse the sprayer and any tools, then wash yourself.

Government pages we used.

Each precaution above traces to a public health or environmental agency and not to our own marketing.

  • CDC prevention guidance for households: Block the entrances first, then clear the old droppings.
  • CDC cleanup page for rodent-fouled spaces: Damp-cleaning directions for households, one move after another.

Garage and storage FAQ.

What makes a garage attractive to rodents?

Shelter, quiet and a pile of stored goods that rarely get disturbed. Cartons, fabric, pet food and birdseed sweeten the deal. The door gasket and the threshold are the usual entries.

Should I move everything out?

Emptying the room is unnecessary. Sliding a few boxes away from the wall so the baseboard shows is enough for the inspection.

What about a refrigerator or freezer in the garage?

Appliances give off warmth, and their compressors sit behind panels where animals can nest. The technician checks behind and under them.

Is a shed handled the same way?

Yes. A shed gets the same look at its door, floor and wall bases, and gaps are closed with the same materials.

Do you handle basements?

A basement or lower-level storage room gets the same treatment where a home has one. Not every house nearby does, so it depends on yours.

What to note about the space.

Can you see light under the roll-up door? What is propped against the walls? Which carton hasn't moved in a year? We ask the same of sheds and utility rooms.
