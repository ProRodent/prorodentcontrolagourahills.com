Ten Questions to Ask Before You Hire Rodent Control

Two companies can quote the same job very differently. The questions below help you judge what each quote includes and what it leaves out.

When you search for rodent control, you will find a range of offers: flat fees, monthly plans, bait-only treatments, trap-only treatments and full programs that include sealing. They can look similar on a website, yet they produce very different results. These ten questions help you sort them out.

Questions about the method.

  • Will you inspect the whole property before quoting?
  • How will you identify the animal and the entry points?
  • Do you seal gaps, and with what materials?
  • What do you use for removal: traps, stations or bait?
  • Are glue boards used?

A sound answer starts with an inspection, adds metal and masonry-grade sealing, and places traps or locked stations on routes the evidence shows. Be wary of plans that rely on bait alone; the EPA page below explains why consumer bait is restricted.

Ask for the estimate by email or paper, and read it before agreeing. The inspection page explains how ours is structured.

Questions about safety.

  • How will devices be kept away from children and pets?
  • How do you handle cleanup of droppings and nests?
  • Do you follow the CDC method for cleanup?

A reliable provider talks about locked stations, careful placement and damp cleanup. The CDC box on this page restates that last method, so you can compare it with what you are told.

Questions about paperwork.

Rules about licensing and insurance vary by location and by the type of work. We make no claims about them on this site. Ask any provider to state in writing what they hold, and check it with the issuing agency if it matters to you.

It is also reasonable to ask how long the provider has served your area and what their process is when a job runs long. Expect a plain answer, not a sales pitch.

The cost of a missing step.

The most common reason a problem returns is an open entry. A quote that skips sealing may look cheaper at first. If animals come back, the savings disappear. The sealing page explains the stage, and the trapping page explains what removal involves.

The second most common reason is an unfinished cleanup. Droppings and soiled insulation hold odors and can draw animals. The sanitizing page covers that stage.

A short comparison worksheet.

  • Write down each provider's name, date and what they inspected.
  • Note whether they gave a written estimate and when.
  • List the stages included and their cost.
  • Mark whether sealing and rechecks are included.
  • Add your own impression of how they answered questions.

Compare the sheets side by side. Look for completeness, not just price.

Federal cleanup advice, condensed.

Many homeowners handle small cleanups themselves, even when a technician is on the way, so keep these precautions nearby.

  • A single dry pellet can crumble at a touch and release a cloud you cannot see.
  • Fresh air thins whatever is hanging in a sealed room, so let it work while nobody is inside.
  • Stash a spare pair at hand so you never pick up a pellet barehanded.
  • Paper towels plus disinfectant spray, and the towels go into a bag at once.
  • Gloves join the other waste after the surface is wiped.
  • Wash your forearms along with your palms.

Hands-on questions.

Should I accept the lowest price?

Not automatically. Compare what is included: inspection, sealing, rechecks and cleanup. A low price that leaves entries open may mean a repeat call.

Is a flat annual plan a good idea?

It depends on the property. For a house with a solved problem, a plan may be more than you need. Ask what each visit includes.

Do I need a written estimate?

Yes. A written estimate lists the findings, the plan and the cost of each stage, and it protects both sides.

What paperwork can I ask for?

Ask for any licenses or insurance the job requires in your area, in writing. We make no claims on this site beyond what a quote states.

Can I get a second opinion?

Yes. A written inspection and estimate from one provider can be reviewed by another.
