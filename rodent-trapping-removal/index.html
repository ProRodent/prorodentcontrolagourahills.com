Position matters more than numbers.

A rodent is a creature of habit. It follows the same wall, beam or pipe night after night, and it avoids open spaces. A trap on that line is much more likely to work than a trap in the middle of a room. For that reason, the first part of any trapping job is reading the evidence, not setting devices.

The technician looks for greasy rub marks, droppings along a path, gnaw marks at the corners of boxes and tracks in dust. Each of those hints at a run, and a trap goes where the runs are strongest.

Every device is placed with the entrance toward the wall, and in a spot where a missed step cannot trip a person. Baits are small and secured so animals cannot carry them off.

What a trapping job looks like in your house.

A walk of the property marks the busiest runs. Devices are placed, photographed and logged, and the plan notes the date for the next visit. Some jobs need a couple of return trips, though a small mouse problem may need fewer.

  • Clear pets and children from the area during placement.
  • Leave devices exactly where they are until the technician returns.
  • Call if a device is sprung or an animal is visible.
  • Never reach into a station or trap with bare hands.

Why trapping alone is not enough.

Traps remove the animals inside, but if the doorway is open, new ones arrive. Sealing, which the exclusion page describes, normally follows once catches show which entries are in use. If the inspection has not happened yet, the inspection page explains how that step feeds the plan.

If the animals have been inside long enough to leave a mess, the attic cleanup page covers what follows. Sequence counts: animals out, gaps shut, cleaning last.

Yards, slopes and outbuildings.

Properties with sheds, detached garages or planted slopes may need devices outside as well as inside. Stations used outdoors are tamper-resistant and anchored down, and they go along fences and foundations rather than out on open grass. Nobody can tell from a map which lots in the area will need them, so the technician decides on site.

While the clock runs.

  • Open a three-foot lane in front of the wall with the most sound.
  • A trap that already holds an animal stays untouched; just say so on the phone.
  • Spraying an aerosol into a wall cavity can send the animal deeper.
  • Jot your questions on a card near the phone.

Protective steps for rodent mess.

Cleaning feels urgent, yet hurrying is the wrong move. The steps here keep dust and germs down and come from federal guidance.

  • Brooms, dusters and compressed air are out; a pump sprayer is in.
  • Attics, garages and sheds hold stale air, so let the breeze flow for half an hour before entering.
  • Skin picks up what gloves block, so wear them before reaching for stained items.
  • Soak the droppings and nests with diluted bleach or a household disinfectant until they shine.
  • Knot the bag firmly and put it in an outdoor bin with a lid.
  • Wash your forearms along with your palms.

Trap-related queries.

Which is better, snap traps or stations?

Snap traps work well for mice and for rats indoors. Enclosed stations suit places where children, pets or wildlife could reach a loose device. The technician chooses by location and explains the choice.

Why not use poison?

A bait-fed animal can die within a wall, leaving behind an odor and a cleanup job. Federal rules also limit consumer products, and the EPA link below explains why. We lean on traps and sealing.

Are glue boards used?

No. They catch non-target animals such as lizards and small birds, and they cause suffering. We steer people away from them.

How many traps will go down?

That depends on the number of animals and the number of routes. The estimate lists the count and the positions.

What happens to the animals caught?

They are removed and disposed of safely. Customers do not have to handle them.

Where traps will be placed.

Say which woodpile, shed, hedge or heap of leaves sits closest to the walls; routes frequently start at one of them. The method does not change from one covered place to the next.
