Dryer Vents, Bath Fans and the Caps That Go Missing

Your house breathes through a half dozen small openings. If one of them has lost its cap, it is a door that you cannot see from the street.

Walk around your house and count the small openings in the wall and the roof: dryer vent, bathroom fan, range hood, attic vents, crawl-space vents, and the cap on each plumbing stack. Every one of them is designed to let air out or in. Every one of them can also let a mouse in if the cover is missing, bent or loose.

The usual suspects.

Start with the ones at ground level, since you can see them without a ladder. Then look at the ones on the roof from the ground, and use binoculars if you have them.

What to look for.

  • A flap that hangs open or no longer closes.
  • A cover that is missing or lying in the planter.
  • A screen that is bent, rusted or pushed in.
  • Droppings, grease marks or shredded material below the vent.
  • Daylight visible from the inside through the opening.

If you see any of these, photograph the vent and note its location. A technician can then decide which material fits.

Why metal beats everything else.

Plastic covers crack in the sun. Mesh sold for screens is often thin enough to chew. Foam and caulk are soft. For vents that must breathe, the best answer is a formed metal hood or screen fastened with screws, and for vents that must stay clear, such as the dryer, a hood with a spring-loaded flap.

Fine mesh on a dryer vent is a fire risk, because lint builds up quickly. Use the right hood, and clean it regularly. The exclusion page explains how each type of opening is handled.

Attic and roof vents.

Gable vents, ridge vents and soffit vents usually have screens to keep out animals and insects. Over time the screens corrode or detach. A rat can pull a loose screen aside, and a mouse can pass through a torn corner.

These repairs often need a ladder and care on the roof. A technician replaces damaged mesh with stainless or galvanized screening that is screwed in, not stapled. If you can only reach the ones at ground level, note which ones you cannot see.

Signs that an animal is already using a vent.

  • Droppings or grease marks on the sill or siding below.
  • Nest material visible in the duct.
  • Scratching sounds that begin or end at the vent.
  • A draft or smell coming from the opening.

Do not stick your hand inside a vent. Photograph what you see and call. If a duct is full of nest material, it needs to be cleaned before use, because blocked ducts can cause fires or moisture problems.

A note about tile and stucco.

Houses with tile roofs have gaps where tiles meet the ridge or the wall. Houses with stucco have seams around penetrations. Whatever material your house uses, the seam where one material meets another is a place to look. Some of those seams close with metal flashing or mortar-grade sealant.

We have no survey of how many homes in Agoura Hills use each material. The technician looks at what you have.

A twice-a-year routine.

  • Walk the exterior in spring and fall.
  • Test each damper by hand or watch it during a run cycle.
  • Replace caps and flaps that are missing or loose.
  • Photograph anything you cannot fix and keep the pictures for the call.

Ten minutes twice a year protects you from a surprising number of problems. If you find gaps, a short call gets a plan going, and the trapping page and inspection page explain what comes next.

Handling droppings without raising dust.

Dried rodent waste breaks into fine particles, so the safe way to deal with it is slow and damp. These steps follow public health guidance and apply even if you never book a visit.

  • A single dry pellet can crumble at a touch and release a cloud you cannot see.
  • Public health pages put airing out first on the list, with roughly thirty minutes as the benchmark.
  • Gloves while working and soap afterward make a pair that protects you.
  • Wet every inch you plan to touch, including the spot underneath the pellets.
  • Seal towels, gloves and waste in a bag, then drop that bag into a second one before disposal.
  • Washing is the final step, whether or not you wore gloves.

Asked after reading.

Can I cover a dryer vent with mesh?

Do not use fine mesh on a dryer vent, because lint clogs it and creates a fire risk. Use a proper vent hood with a flap, and clean it regularly.

How often should I check vent caps?

Twice a year, in spring and fall, and after any strong wind.

Which vents matter most?

Dryer, bathroom fan, range hood, attic and crawl-space vents. Any with a missing or damaged cover is a risk.

Does a bent screen count as a gap?

Yes. A mouse can pass through a hole about the size of a pencil, and bent mesh often leaves one.

Can I fix these myself?

Replacing a hood or a cap is within reach for many homeowners. Roof vents and anything at height are better left to a professional.
