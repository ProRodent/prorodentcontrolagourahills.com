Gaps are the whole game.

Removal of animals without sealing the entry points is like emptying a bathtub with the tap still running. Rodents will return as long as a gap exists, and a young mouse can pass through an opening roughly the width of a pencil. A rat needs a slightly larger hole, but it can gnaw to enlarge one.

That is why materials matter. A tube of caulk looks like a solution and lasts about as long as a night. Metal mesh, flashing and mortar-grade sealant resist the teeth that defeat softer products.

Where gaps hide.

  • Eave returns and the seam between the roof edge and the wall.
  • Gable, ridge and soffit vents with torn or bent screens.
  • Dryer, bath fan and range hood exhausts missing caps or flaps.
  • Utility penetrations: gas line, hose bib, cable and AC refrigerant lines.
  • Garage door corners, bottom seals and the sill beneath them.
  • Weep holes and foundation vents, especially in older or settled areas.

Hillside homes and houses with additions often show more seams. We cannot say which ones in town do, so the technician checks all of them.

Timing the work.

The sealing step is scheduled after the trapping has shown which entries are in use. If the doors are closed too early, animals trapped inside find another way out, often through a wall or ceiling. The technician times the final closures to when the structure is clear.

Once the exterior is shut, the interior may still need cleanup. The attic cleanup page covers that stage, and the repair page explains what happens when gnawing has damaged materials.

A home-maintenance walk you can do yourself.

Once or twice a year, walk the outside with a flashlight. Look at the base of the garage door, the screens on vents and the seams around pipes. Anything you can slip a pencil into deserves a call. Seasonal checks are easier than emergency repairs.

If the exterior has stucco, tile or siding, look at the points where different materials meet. Those seams are where movement opens gaps over time.

Between the call and the knock.

  • Keep the noisiest room closed and let the household know why.
  • Leave the broom in the closet; bristles throw settled grit into the air.
  • Pack away open bait boxes where small hands cannot reach.
  • Note on a pad when each scratch or thump happens.

Federal cleanup advice, condensed.

When no crew can come right away, this box covers what is safe to do on your own and what should wait for protective gear.

  • Pellets and nests turn to powder when dry, so no broom and no shop vac.
  • Public health pages put airing out first on the list, with roughly thirty minutes as the benchmark.
  • Do not touch nesting material with bare fingers, even if it looks dry.
  • Federal guidance suggests a bleach mix of 1:9 with water, prepared on the day.
  • Two bags keep the waste contained until pickup.
  • Fever or aches in the weeks after contact are worth mentioning to a doctor, along with the rodent exposure.

Sealing queries from callers.

Why not just use spray foam?

Rodents gnaw through foam with ease, and it conceals a gap instead of closing it. It may back up metal mesh but must never stand alone.

How do you find the gaps?

By following the evidence. Rub marks, droppings and gnaw marks lead to the openings, and the technician then walks the exterior looking for others the animals could use.

Do vents get blocked?

No. Vents need airflow. Instead they get metal screening or formed covers that keep animals out and let air through.

Will sealing trap animals inside?

It could, so sealing is scheduled for after the animals have gone. The structure is confirmed empty before the final seals are fixed.

Is sealing guaranteed?

No work against living animals can be promised without limits. The return visit tests the repairs, and any failure is addressed.

Scoping a sealing job.

Describe the roof style, the vents, the door seals and any branch brushing the shingles. Each building is priced on the openings it actually has.
