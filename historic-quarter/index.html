What we know about the Historic Quarter, and what we do not.

Published material about Agoura Hills mentions the Historic Quarter in a single photo caption, describing a view from its southern edge. Nothing else came with it: no list of streets, no building details, no dates. This page uses the name as people use it, keeps factual claims to a minimum and sticks to rodent advice for a home or shop.

What it can offer is practical guidance for any older or mixed-use building. If yours fits, the checklists below apply. If it does not, the technician adapts.

What older buildings often share.

This is general reasoning, not a description of any particular street. Buildings of earlier eras may have crawl spaces rather than slabs, wood-framed windows and doors that no longer sit tight, and additions that left seams between old and new framing. Those features can leave gaps animals use.

  • Crawl-space vents with broken or missing screens.
  • Door thresholds and wood sills that have worn or shrunk.
  • Seams where an addition meets the original wall.
  • Old chimneys or flues without caps.
  • Utility lines added later with loose sleeves.

Preserving original features matters to many owners. The sealing page explains how metal mesh and flashing can be fitted in ways that protect the building, and the technician will ask what you want kept intact.

If the building mixes uses.

A district known as historic may include homes, offices and shops side by side. We cannot say whether the quarter does. If your building has an apartment over a shop or shares a wall with a business, tell us. Plumbing chases and shared walls can act as vertical highways.

Crawl spaces and quiet rooms.

Spaces that nobody visits are the ones animals favor. A crawl space or storage closet that stays shut for months can host a nest long before anyone notices. If your building has one, mention it, and clear a path to the access door before the visit.

  • Check crawl-space vents and the access door from outside.
  • Look for droppings at the base of the access hatch.
  • Photograph anything chewed or stained.
  • Keep stored goods off the floor.

Holding steps until the crew arrives.

Do not handle droppings, and keep staff, customers and children away from the area. Skip bait, because a poisoned animal can die inside a wall, and sticky boards trap other wildlife. If cleaning cannot wait, use the method in the CDC box below.

When you phone, mention the room, the hour and your first clue. Short is fine.

How the visit would run.

Fresh evidence leads the way. Then comes a lap of the exterior, a log of openings and devices set on actual runs. Sealing holds back until the first catches show which entries are live, and a second visit checks the work. It mirrors the rest of Agoura Hills, fitted to your building's age and use.

Next steps for tonight.

  • Open a three-foot lane in front of the wall with the most sound.
  • A trap that already holds an animal stays untouched; just say so on the phone.
  • Spraying an aerosol into a wall cavity can send the animal deeper.
  • Jot your questions on a card near the phone.

Federal cleanup advice, condensed.

Cleaning feels urgent, yet hurrying is the wrong move. The steps here keep dust and germs down and come from federal guidance.

  • Brooms, dusters and compressed air are out; a pump sprayer is in.
  • Make a draft with two openings, then busy yourself elsewhere until the time is up.
  • Slip on rubber, latex or vinyl gloves before handling anything soiled, and rinse hands afterward.
  • Wet every inch you plan to touch, including the spot underneath the pellets.
  • Gloves join the other waste after the surface is wiped.
  • Wash your forearms along with your palms.
