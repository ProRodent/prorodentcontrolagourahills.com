A business is a building with a schedule.

A shop, office or restaurant has activity patterns a house does not: deliveries at dawn, trash at night, doors propped for loading. Rodents notice those patterns. The plan begins with questions about how the building is used, because the answers say where animals are likely to get in and where they find food.

The first visit is an inspection. The technician walks the exterior, the receiving area, storage rooms and any kitchen, noting gaps, stored goods and trash handling. The written report lists findings and proposed actions.

Common weak points.

  • Rear and loading doors with gaps or propped open during deliveries.
  • Cardboard and pallets stacked against walls.
  • Floor drains, pipe penetrations and gaps around equipment.
  • Dumpsters and recycling carts parked against the building.
  • Ceiling spaces and shared walls with neighbors.

None of these is a flaw in the building. They are habits and features worth checking.

Evenings, events and busy locations.

The Canyon Club, a concert venue in Agoura Hills, reminds us that popular evening spots generate crowds, deliveries and trash well after dark. How any given venue runs is unknown to us. The lesson is general: businesses near events should check bins, back doors and storage when they close.

A brief closing walk helps any business: look along the base of each wall, store goods off the floor and take trash out before locking up.

Reports and follow-up.

A report closes every visit, noting findings, completed work and points to watch before the next one. Owners, managers and landlords can keep it as a record, and it eases conversations with neighbors about shared features.

Pricing is set after the first inspection. The estimate lists the scheduled visits, the sealing work and any cleanup, so you see what you are paying for.

Tasks for the wait.

  • Mute the television for a few minutes and listen at the wall that sounds worst.
  • Never run a vacuum across droppings, however few.
  • Look at pantry boxes for gnaw marks and move open food into jars or hard bins.
  • Count the places where fresh signs appear and tell us the tally.

Handling droppings without raising dust.

Hantavirus and other germs can ride on dust from dried waste, and that is why the method below avoids anything that stirs it up.

  • A single dry pellet can crumble at a touch and release a cloud you cannot see.
  • Open every window you can reach, then step outside for a half hour.
  • Choose gloves long enough to cover the wrist, where dust likes to land.
  • Soak the droppings and nests with diluted bleach or a household disinfectant until they shine.
  • Two bags keep the waste contained until pickup.
  • Wash your forearms along with your palms.

What business owners wonder.

Do you work after hours?

Yes, by arrangement. The call determines timing, and the line is answered at any hour.

What do the reports contain?

Findings, actions taken, devices placed or checked, and recommendations. They are written in plain English.

Can I share the report with an inspector?

You may. The report is yours, and it can document pest management for your own records.

What if the problem comes from a neighbor?

Shared walls and dumpsters are noted. We can describe what we found, though we cannot enforce action next door.

Do you handle restaurants and food service?

Yes. Kitchens, dry storage, receiving areas and trash rooms are checked, with devices placed away from food contact surfaces.
