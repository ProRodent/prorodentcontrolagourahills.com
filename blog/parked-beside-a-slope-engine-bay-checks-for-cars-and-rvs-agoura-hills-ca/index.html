Parked Beside a Slope: Engine Bay Checks for Cars and RVs

A warm engine, a quiet driveway and a pile of leaves nearby make a vehicle a tempting den. A two-minute check can save a very expensive repair.

Cars, trucks, RVs and trailers sit unused more than most people realize. A commuter vehicle might rest all weekend. A project car might sit for months. A camper in the side yard might wait for the next trip. In every case, the warm engine bay and the quiet, sheltered spaces beneath the hood and the body are attractive to rodents.

Why vehicles draw rodents.

A recently driven engine gives off heat for hours. Air filter housings, wiring looms and insulation panels offer soft material for nesting. A vehicle parked near a hedge, a slope or a pile of leaves offers an easy approach. And plastic coatings on wires may taste good or simply be convenient to chew.

We cannot say how many vehicles in Agoura Hills are affected, and we make no claim about any particular street. Some properties sit beside planted slopes or open hillside, and parking a vehicle there might shorten a rodent's trip. The checks below apply anywhere.

A two-minute engine bay check.

  • Open the hood and look at the air filter box for shredded material or seeds.
  • Check the top of the battery and the wiring looms for chewed insulation.
  • Look for droppings on the engine cover or the strut towers.
  • Smell for ammonia or a musty odor.
  • Check the cabin air filter, which is often behind the glove box.

Use a flashlight and wear gloves. If you see a nest touching hot parts, do not start the engine.

Where you park changes the odds.

Parking on a hard surface away from hedges, woodpiles and stored boxes helps. A garage with a good door seal is better still. If you must park on the street or beside planting, check more often.

  • Keep leaves, brush and stored items away from the parking spot.
  • Do not leave food or wrappers in the cabin.
  • Consider parking nose-out so you can see the front quickly.
  • Open the hood on a vehicle that has been stored.

RVs, trailers and project cars.

Larger vehicles have more cavities. Look at the wheel wells, the underside, the propane compartment, the storage bays and the area under the bed. Run a flashlight along wiring runs and plumbing lines. Cover openings with metal mesh where you can.

A vehicle parked for a whole season should be emptied of food and cleaned inside. A battery maintainer may help, and a friend can start the engine and peek under the hood from time to time.

After the nest is gone.

Have a mechanic or auto electrician inspect for damage. Gnawed wiring can cause trouble that only shows up later. Keep photos of the nest and any damaged parts, and ask for a written estimate if you plan to make an insurance claim.

The next step is making the parking area less appealing. The yard page explains how outdoor cover is reduced, and the sealing page explains how garages and sheds are closed up.

A habit that pays.

Look under the hood every time you check the oil, or once a week for a vehicle that sits. It costs two minutes and can prevent a repair bill that runs to hundreds of dollars. If you find signs, call. A short description of what you saw is enough to get started.

Protective steps for rodent mess.

Dried rodent waste breaks into fine particles, so the safe way to deal with it is slow and damp. These steps follow public health guidance and apply even if you never book a visit.

  • A shop vacuum on a dusty pile is a bad idea; a leaf blower is worse.
  • Plan on half an hour of airing before any wiping begins.
  • Slip on rubber, latex or vinyl gloves before handling anything soiled, and rinse hands afterward.
  • Wet every inch you plan to touch, including the spot underneath the pellets.
  • Knot the bag firmly and put it in an outdoor bin with a lid.
  • Wash your forearms along with your palms.

Asked after reading.

How often should I check the engine bay?

If a vehicle sits for more than a few days, look under the hood before you start it. For vehicles parked near brush, check weekly.

What if I find a nest?

Do not start the engine. Wear gloves, and call. A technician removes the nest, and a mechanic checks for damage.

Do peppermint oil and similar products work?

Evidence is mixed, and smells fade. We do not rely on them. Removing nests and parking away from cover is more dependable.

Can rodents damage an RV or trailer?

Yes. Storage bays, wheel wells, under-bed areas and wiring runs are all places to check.

Will insurance cover rodent damage to a car?

Coverage varies by policy, so ask your insurer, and save photos along with written repair estimates.
